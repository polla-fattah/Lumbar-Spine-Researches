# **AI-Based Lumbar Spine Classification for Automated Degenerative Disease Analysis Using MRI: A Comprehensive Literature Review**

## **Introduction to Lumbar Degenerative Diseases and Diagnostic Imaging**

### **The Clinical and Socioeconomic Burden of Lumbar Degeneration**

Lumbar degenerative disc disease (DDD) represents one of the most pervasive and debilitating musculoskeletal disorders globally, imposing a profound socioeconomic burden and significantly diminishing the quality of life for affected populations1. The pathogenesis of lumbar degeneration is inherently multifactorial, driven by chronological aging, chronic mechanical stress, genetic predispositions, and lifestyle factors such as occupational hazards and obesity3. Morphologically, the degenerative cascade initiates within the intervertebral discs (IVDs), which serve as the primary cartilaginous shock absorbers between adjacent vertebral bodies1. As these structures dehydrate and lose their proteoglycan matrix—a process accelerating from the third decade of life onward—they suffer a progressive loss of vertical height, structural integrity, and biomechanical resilience3.  
This initial disc dehydration and narrowing invariably triggers a domino effect of compensatory structural alterations throughout the lumbar spine, specifically spanning the five primary intervertebral disc levels: L1/L2, L2/L3, L3/L4, L4/L5, and L5/S15. These alterations include the outward bulging or frank herniation of the nucleus pulposus through the weakened annulus fibrosus, the hypertrophy of the ligamentum flavum, and osteoarthritic changes (osteophyte formation) within the posterior facet joints4. Cumulatively, these morphological changes encroach upon the critical neurovascular spaces of the spinal column, culminating in a broad clinical classification known as Lumbar Spinal Stenosis (LSS)9.  
Lumbar spinal stenosis is primarily categorized anatomically into three distinct forms of narrowing, each correlating with specific, often debilitating, symptomatology:

> 1. **Spinal Canal Stenosis (SCS):** The concentric or eccentric narrowing of the central vertebral canal, which houses the thecal sac and the cauda equina6. Severe SCS frequently presents as neurogenic claudication—a bilateral cramping and weakness in the lower extremities exacerbated by walking and relieved by lumbar flexion.  
> 2. **Neural Foraminal Narrowing (NFN):** The constriction of the lateral neural foramina, the bony windows through which the exiting spinal nerve roots traverse9. NFN typically results in unilateral or bilateral radiculopathy, characterized by sharp, shooting pain, paresthesia, and motor deficits radiating down a specific dermatomal distribution.  
> 3. **Subarticular Stenosis (SAS):** Also known as lateral recess stenosis, this involves the narrowing of the subarticular zones located medially to the neural foramina, compressing the traversing nerve roots before they exit the spine at the subsequent level6.

The epidemiological prevalence of low back pain and associated radiculopathy is staggering. In the United States alone, the direct medical expenditures and indirect socioeconomic costs—stemming from lost productivity, disability claims, and prolonged pharmacological management—are estimated to range between $50 billion and $100 billion annually9. Consequently, the accurate, timely, and anatomically localized diagnosis of lumbar stenosis and disc degeneration is paramount for guiding clinical triage. Therapeutic pathways diverge significantly based on the exact morphological severity, ranging from conservative physical therapy and epidural steroid injections to highly invasive surgical interventions, such as laminectomies or spinal fusion1.

### **Diagnostic Utility of Multiplanar and Multisequence MRI**

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) serves as the definitive gold standard for the non-invasive evaluation of the lumbar spine. Unlike Computed Tomography (CT) or plain radiography, which excel primarily at visualizing osseous structures but offer poor soft-tissue delineation, MRI provides unparalleled soft-tissue contrast1. This modality enables the detailed visualization of intervertebral discs, the thecal sac, individual nerve roots, and ligamentous structures without exposing the patient to potentially harmful ionizing radiation2.  
Standard clinical diagnostic workflows for the lumbar spine are inherently multiplanar and multisequence. Because the spinal column is a complex, three-dimensional anatomical structure, no single imaging plane or sequence can definitively capture all pathological variations. Radiologists systematically analyze a suite of MR sequences, each optimized through specific magnetic relaxation times (T1 and T2) and radiofrequency pulse applications to highlight distinct tissues and pathologies5.

| MRI Sequence | Imaging Plane | Primary Clinical Utility and Tissue Contrast | Target Pathologies and Stenosis Types |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **T2-Weighted (T2W)** | Sagittal (Side View) | Provides hyperintense (bright) signals for fluids, highlighting the hydration status of the nucleus pulposus and the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) within the thecal sac against darker ligamentous tissues. | Spinal Canal Stenosis (SCS), Disc Degeneration (Pfirrmann Grading), Central Disc Extrusions6. |
| **STIR / Fat-Suppressed** | Sagittal (Side View) | Utilizes Short Tau Inversion Recovery to suppress the hyperintense signal of adipose tissue, drastically enhancing the visibility of tissue edema, inflammation, and bone marrow lesions. | Active inflammatory processes, Modic endplate changes, severe canal stenosis, neoplastic infiltrations5. |
| **T1-Weighted (T1W)** | Sagittal (Side View) | Optimizes longitudinal relaxation to produce bright signals for fat. This provides excellent anatomical delineation of the epidural fat surrounding the exiting nerve roots within the bony neural foramina. | Neural Foraminal Narrowing (NFN), osseous abnormalities, foraminal disc herniations, fat infiltration6. |
| **T2-Weighted (T2W)** | Axial (Cross-Sectional) | Captures transverse cross-sections through the intervertebral discs and vertebral bodies, providing a definitive view of the central canal area, lateral recesses, and traversing nerve roots. | Subarticular Stenosis (SAS), lateral recess stenosis, exact lateralization of disc herniation, facet joint hypertrophy5. |

### **Fundamental Clinical Grading Systems**

To interpret the immense volume of data generated by multisequence MRI protocols, the radiological and orthopedic communities rely on standardized clinical grading systems. These systems abstract complex morphological changes into discrete, ordinal categories of severity, forming the foundational ground truth upon which modern artificial intelligence models are trained.  
The Pfirrmann grading system, formally introduced in 2001, is the seminal and most universally adopted framework for classifying intervertebral disc degeneration. The system relies entirely on sagittal T2-weighted MRI sequences. It classifies the severity of degeneration on a five-point ordinal scale (Grades I to V) based on four primary morphological criteria: the structural homogeneity of the disc, the visual distinction between the inner nucleus pulposus and the outer annulus fibrosus, the overall signal intensity (correlating with tissue hydration), and the preservation of disc height8. While foundational, the Pfirrmann system specifically evaluates intrinsic disc health rather than the downstream compressive effects on neurovascular structures.  
To directly assess spinal stenosis, researchers have developed specialized grading frameworks, such as those pioneered by Lee et al. (2010, 2011). These systems provide structured, visually intuitive criteria for grading neural foraminal stenosis on sagittal T1-weighted images and central canal stenosis on axial T2-weighted images. The severity is generally categorized into distinct tiers: Normal (no root compression and normal epidural fat), Mild (partial obliteration of perineural fat without obvious nerve root morphological changes), Moderate (near-complete obliteration of fat with some root compression), and Severe (complete obliteration of the foraminal space or central canal with severe morphologic collapse of the nerve root).  
Despite the widespread implementation of these standardized frameworks, a critical clinical bottleneck persists: manual MRI interpretation remains a highly labor-intensive, subjective, and fatigue-prone process1. Extensive literature documenting inter-observer and intra-observer agreement has repeatedly demonstrated significant variability even among subspecialty-trained neuroradiologists9. This variability—particularly in differentiating between borderline mild-to-moderate or moderate-to-severe cases—engenders pronounced inconsistencies in diagnostic confidence, epidemiological reporting, and subsequent surgical planning1.  
The overwhelming workload generated by an aging global population requiring lumbar MRIs, combined with the urgent clinical necessity for objective, highly reproducible, and standardized severity grading, has catalyzed a profound paradigm shift. This shift has driven the medical imaging community to aggressively pursue the integration of artificial intelligence (AI), specifically automated machine learning and deep learning pipelines, to mitigate diagnostic subjectivity and streamline modern clinical workflows1.

## **Evolution of AI in Spinal Imaging**

The chronological trajectory of automated lumbar spine image analysis is defined by a continuous pursuit of higher feature extraction capability, moving from rigid, classical computer vision techniques toward highly dynamic, data-driven deep learning architectures. Understanding this evolution is critical, as it highlights the persistent challenges in medical imaging—chiefly, the extreme variability of human anatomy and the necessity for algorithms to learn robust, generalized representations of disease.

### **The Era of Traditional Machine Learning**

Prior to the proliferation of deep neural networks, early attempts to automate the diagnosis of spinal pathologies relied heavily on traditional machine learning paradigms. These conventional methodologies necessitated manual feature engineering—a laborious and computationally brittle process wherein domain experts explicitly defined mathematical representations of visual data. Researchers utilized classical computer vision algorithms to extract handcrafted features from localized regions of MRI scans. Commonly employed algorithms included Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) for capturing edge structures, Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrices (GLCM) for quantifying tissue texture and homogeneity, and specialized active contour models (snakes) for tracing the boundaries of vertebral bodies and intervertebral discs8.  
Once extracted, these numerical feature vectors were subsequently fed into traditional classification algorithms, most notably Support Vector Machines (SVMs), Random Forests, or shallow Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs)8. While these foundational models successfully demonstrated the theoretical feasibility of automated image analysis in radiology, their clinical utility was fundamentally constrained. Handcrafted features were highly susceptible to minor variations in MRI hardware, differing magnetic field strengths (e.g., 1.5 Tesla vs. 3.0 Tesla), slice thicknesses, patient positioning, and inherent anatomical differences among patients (such as differing degrees of lumbar lordosis or scoliosis). Consequently, these early systems suffered from high false-positive rates and failed to generalize when deployed on external datasets outside their limited training distributions.

### **The Deep Learning Breakthrough: Early Milestone Frameworks**

The integration of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) initiated a critical inflection point in medical image analysis, rendering manual feature extraction obsolete. CNNs operate through representation learning, automatically discovering hierarchical feature representations directly from raw pixel data through successive layers of convolutions, spatial pooling, and non-linear activation functions (such as ReLUs)8. In the domain of spinal imaging, this transition yielded unprecedented improvements in diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, allowing automated systems to routinely match or exceed human expert performance in controlled settings4.  
A watershed moment in this transition was the introduction of SpineNet by Jamaludin, Kadir, and Zisserman (2017). SpineNet represented one of the very first comprehensive, end-to-end deep learning frameworks capable of the automated radiological grading of clinical spinal MRIs. Operating primarily on sagittal T2-weighted MRI sequences, SpineNet achieved exceptional performance in classifying Pfirrmann disc degeneration and disc narrowing. Crucially, the authors addressed one of the primary criticisms of deep learning in medicine—the "black box" phenomenon—by incorporating evidence visualization. The network generated localized heatmaps overlaying the original MRI, explicitly highlighting the specific anatomical regions (such as a dehydrated nucleus pulposus or a compromised endplate) that drove the model's severity predictions. Furthermore, SpineNet leveraged longitudinal MRI data to perform self-supervised representation learning before fine-tuning, demonstrating that CNNs could deduce robust, generalized anatomical representations without requiring exhaustive manual pixel-level annotation for every training epoch.  
Following SpineNet, the DeepSPINE framework introduced by Lu et al. (2018) established an architectural philosophy that continues to dominate state-of-the-art medical image analysis today: the absolute necessity of anatomical localization prior to severity classification14. When a standard CNN is fed an entire, uncropped sagittal or axial MRI slice, the actual region of pathological interest—for instance, the L4/L5 intervertebral disc and its adjacent neural foramina—occupies merely a fraction of the total pixel space. Extraneous soft tissues, abdominal organs, background noise, and varying spinal curvatures can easily confound a global image classifier.  
DeepSPINE addressed this by utilizing a rigorous, multi-stage modular approach. The framework first deployed a semantic segmentation architecture (specifically, a U-Net) to perform precise vertebral segmentation and disc-level designation14. By explicitly localizing and extracting bounding boxes centered on the five lumbar discs (L1-S1), the system cropped out irrelevant data. The localized image patches were then fed into a secondary, independent classification network dedicated exclusively to grading spinal stenosis14. This decoupling of localization and classification allowed the classification network to dedicate its entire parameter capacity to evaluating the subtle, high-frequency morphological changes indicative of stenosis, drastically improving diagnostic accuracy and reducing false positives14.

### **Interpretability and Clinical Validation**

As deep learning models achieved high accuracy in controlled settings, the academic focus naturally expanded toward interpretability and independent clinical validation—crucial prerequisites for actual deployment in hospital ecosystems. Recent literature explicitly highlights this transition. For instance, subsequent studies focused on the external validation of SpineNet (McSweeney et al., 2023\) and its successor SpineNetV2 (Wu et al., 2026), rigorously testing these open-source frameworks on entirely novel patient cohorts to quantify generalization drop-off.  
Simultaneously, researchers such as Bharadwaj et al. (2023) pushed the boundaries of interpretable classification. By developing frameworks that provide explicit, pixel-level attribution for central stenosis, foraminal stenosis, and facet arthropathy from axial MRIs, these systems allow human radiologists to rapidly audit the AI's logic. Furthermore, recent studies (e.g., Cheng et al., 2026; Lim et al., 2022\) have begun evaluating human-machine assistance paradigms, proving that deep learning-assisted reporting significantly improves both the speed and the standardization of clinical lumbar spine MRI workflows. This body of literature confirms that the field has moved far beyond the simple binary classification of disease presence; contemporary research demands anatomically localized, multi-task, explainable systems capable of discerning subtle gradients of severity across the entire lumbar spine4.

## **State-of-the-Art Architectures in Medical Image Analysis**

The contemporary landscape of automated lumbar spine classification is currently defined by a rigorous and dynamic competition between advanced Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and the recently emergent paradigm of Vision Transformers (ViTs). Both architectural families offer fundamentally distinct approaches to processing spatial information, extracting hierarchical features, and modeling the complex anatomical relationships inherent to multi-slice MRI sequences. Understanding their distinct mechanisms, advantages, and limitations is central to optimizing computational efficiency and diagnostic accuracy.

### **Convolutional Neural Networks: ResNet50 and EfficientNet-B4**

For nearly a decade, CNNs have served as the undisputed workhorses of medical image analysis. Their architecture is inherently designed to exploit the spatial topologies of images. CNNs operate under the principle of translation invariance and local connectivity; a convolutional filter scans across an image, calculating localized dot products to identify specific visual features (e.g., edges, textures, intensity gradients) regardless of where they appear in the frame. By stacking multiple convolutional layers, CNNs create a hierarchical feature pyramid, gradually assembling low-level edges into complex, highly abstract representations of human anatomy5.  
**ResNet50 (Residual Networks):** As researchers attempted to extract more complex features by building deeper neural networks, they encountered the vanishing gradient problem. During backpropagation, the gradients used to update the network's weights would exponentially diminish as they propagated backward through dozens of layers, effectively arresting the learning process in the foundational layers of the model. ResNet50 resolved this through the introduction of residual blocks containing "skip connections"5. These connections allow the input of a layer to bypass one or more subsequent convolutional layers and be added directly to their output. This architectural innovation provides a direct "superhighway" for gradients to flow unimpeded through the network, enabling the stable training of a deep, 50-layer architecture5.  
Within the context of lumbar MRI analysis, ResNet50 is frequently employed for its robust, reliable feature extraction capabilities. It is the dominant backbone utilized in object detection frameworks—such as Faster R-CNN or YOLOX—employed during the initial localization stage of multi-stage pipelines to identify and bound the five intervertebral discs6. Its deep hierarchical structure is remarkably adept at identifying the localized narrowing of the spinal canal, the displacement of nerve roots, and the subtle hypertrophy of the ligamentum flavum.  
**EfficientNet-B4:**  
While ResNet prioritized depth, subsequent architectures sought a more optimal balance of network dimensions to maximize accuracy while minimizing computational cost. Traditional CNNs typically scaled up by independently increasing network depth (adding more layers), width (increasing the number of channels per layer), or input image resolution. EfficientNet revolutionized this process by introducing a compound scaling method. Using a baseline network optimized via neural architecture search, EfficientNet uniformly scales depth, width, and resolution using a fixed set of mathematically derived scaling coefficients.  
The resulting architecture, particularly variants like EfficientNet-B4 and B5, achieves superior feature extraction capabilities with significantly fewer parameters and lower Floating-Point Operations Per Second (FLOPs) compared to excessively deep or wide legacy networks. In the highly localized task of lumbar stenosis classification, EfficientNet-B4 has demonstrated exceptional performance. When processing tightly cropped regions of interest (ROIs) centered on specific lumbar discs, the architecture efficiently captures the minute pixel intensity variations in T2-weighted axial slices that denote subarticular stenosis or lateral recess obliteration17.  
Despite their ubiquitous success, traditional CNNs suffer from an inherent architectural limitation: a restricted receptive field. The convolution operation is fundamentally local. While deeper layers aggregate these local features into broader representations, CNNs struggle to explicitly model long-range, global contextual dependencies across an entire image—such as the biomechanical relationship, alignment, and compensatory load-bearing between the L1/L2 disc and the L5/S1 disc.

### **The Paradigm Shift to Vision Transformers: The Swin Transformer**

To address the limitations of local receptive fields and enhance global spatial awareness, the medical imaging community has recently witnessed a profound paradigm shift toward Vision Transformers (ViTs). Originally engineered for Natural Language Processing (NLP), Transformers completely eschew convolutional layers. Instead, they rely entirely on self-attention mechanisms to dynamically weigh the importance of every part of the input sequence relative to every other part, regardless of their physical distance within the data structure. When applied to computer vision, an image is divided into discrete, non-overlapping patches, flattened, and treated as a sequential input, similar to words in a sentence.  
However, standard isotropic Vision Transformers face a critical computational bottleneck: the time and memory complexity of global self-attention scales quadratically with the number of image patches (i.e., image resolution). Because clinical MRIs require high spatial resolution to delineate tiny structures like nerve roots, standard ViTs are computationally prohibitive for routine medical image processing.  
The **Swin Transformer (Shifted Window Transformer)** was specifically introduced to resolve this quadratic bottleneck, serving as a highly efficient, general-purpose backbone for computer vision tasks18. The Swin Transformer introduces two critical innovations to the standard ViT architecture:

> 1. **Hierarchical Feature Maps:** Unlike standard ViTs that maintain a constant resolution throughout all layers, the Swin Transformer utilizes Patch Merging layers to progressively merge neighboring patches as the network deepens. This creates a hierarchical feature pyramid similar to traditional CNNs, allowing the network to capture both fine-grained local details and high-level abstract structures18.  
> 2. **Shifted Window Self-Attention:** The architecture calculates self-attention only locally, within predefined, non-overlapping windows that partition the image. To enable cross-window connections and model global context, the window partitioning is explicitly shifted by half a window size between successive self-attention layers (alternating between W-MSA and SW-MSA modules). This shifting mechanism allows information to bleed across boundaries, achieving global modeling while reducing the computational complexity from quadratic to linear relative to the image size18.

In the context of lumbar spine classification, the Swin Transformer provides a profound diagnostic advantage. It simultaneously captures the highly localized, fine-grained anatomical details of a specific intervertebral disc within a local window (e.g., an annular tear or foraminal obliteration) while dynamically mapping the broader, global structural patterns of the entire spinal column via the shifted windows and hierarchical merging18. Architectures integrating the Swin Transformer, such as Swin-Unet, have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance in complex spinal cord segmentation benchmarks by leveraging the benefits of both convolutional-like hierarchical extraction and global attention18.  
Furthermore, hybrid models like SymTC (Symbiotic Transformer-CNN) have been explicitly engineered to merge the local feature extraction prowess of CNNs with the global contextual awareness of Transformers. These hybrid networks integrate novel relative position embeddings into the self-attention modules, dramatically enhancing spatial awareness and topological mapping in complex lumbar MRIs19. In practical applications, particularly within competitive benchmark environments, pre-trained Swin Transformer models have been successfully fine-tuned for multi-class severity prediction tasks (Normal/Mild, Moderate, Severe). Research indicates that sequentially transferring weights—for instance, fine-tuning a Swin Transformer on Spinal Canal Stenosis (SCS) and subsequently utilizing those weights to initialize a model for Subarticular Stenosis (SAS)—significantly boosts performance on smaller datasets, compensating for the Transformer's inherent lack of convolutional inductive biases6.  
However, the debate regarding the optimal architectural choice remains fiercely active. Some rigorous comparative studies analyzing severity grading across the full lumbar spine note that highly optimized, modern convolutional architectures (such as ConvNeXt) can sometimes outpace Swin Transformers in terms of raw computational efficiency (recall per FLOPs) and stability on highly imbalanced datasets, maintaining the ongoing tension between local feature extraction and global context modeling10.

## **Addressing Class Imbalance in Medical Datasets**

A pervasive, ubiquitous, and critically limiting challenge in the development of robust clinical AI models is the extreme class imbalance inherent to medical imaging datasets. In the context of lumbar degenerative diseases, the epidemiological reality of a general clinical population dictates that the vast majority of evaluated intervertebral disc levels will exhibit normal anatomy or merely mild, age-appropriate degenerative changes. Conversely, moderate and severe pathological cases—which are the primary targets for clinical intervention—constitute a distinct, often minute, statistical minority20.  
This reality is directly and starkly mirrored in large-scale benchmark datasets. For instance, in the comprehensive RSNA LumbarDISC dataset, the distribution of spinal canal stenosis (SCS) grades is heavily skewed: an overwhelming 85.4% of labeled cases are classified as Normal/Mild, 8.8% as Moderate, and only 5.9% as Severe5. Similar, and occasionally more severe, imbalances persist across neural foraminal and subarticular grading distributions, where Normal/Mild cases routinely comprise roughly 70% to 78% of the data, and Severe cases account for merely 4% to 11%9.  
When deep learning models—whether based on CNNs or Vision Transformers—are trained on such heavily skewed distributions using standard objective functions (e.g., standard categorical cross-entropy loss), they predictably rapidly converge on a suboptimal local minimum. The models become statistically biased toward constantly predicting the majority class (Normal/Mild), because doing so mathematically minimizes the overall loss across the dataset. This phenomenon yields artificially high overall classification accuracy (e.g., 85% accuracy achieved simply by guessing "Normal" every time) while entirely failing to detect the minority classes13. In a clinical triage setting, this manifests as a dangerously high false-negative rate for severe stenosis—a catastrophic failure, as these are precisely the patients requiring urgent clinical evaluation or decompressive surgery.  
To systematically counteract this imbalance and force the networks to learn meaningful features across all severity levels, the literature relies on several sophisticated, multi-tiered data-level and algorithm-level strategies:

### **Data-Level Interventions: Weighted Sampling and Augmentation**

At the fundamental data pipeline level, weighted random sampling is frequently employed to balance the minibatches during the training phase. Instead of drawing image samples uniformly from the dataset, the probability of selecting an image from a minority class (e.g., Severe stenosis) is artificially inflated. Consequently, each training batch presents the network with a relatively uniform distribution of Normal, Moderate, and Severe levels, preventing the gradients from being entirely dominated by the majority class.  
Furthermore, aggressive data augmentation techniques are applied extensively to the minority classes. Because there are fewer unique severe cases, models are prone to simply memorizing these specific images (overfitting) rather than learning the underlying morphological features of severe stenosis. Researchers employ techniques such as contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) to enhance soft-tissue contrast, alongside random scaling, affine rotations, elastic transformations, and advanced synthesis methods like CutMix or MixUp5. These augmentations artificially expand the geometric and pixel-intensity variance of the severe cases, forcing the model to learn robust, generalized representations of nerve root compression rather than spurious background artifacts17.

### **Algorithmic Interventions: Class-Weighted and Focal Loss**

The most prevalent algorithmic intervention in modern spinal AI literature is the implementation of Class-Weighted Cross-Entropy (WCE) loss. Standard cross-entropy treats all errors equally. WCE modifies the loss function by multiplying the calculated error for a specific class by a predefined scalar weight factor. This forces the backpropagation optimizer to heavily penalize misclassifications of minority classes.  
In standardized evaluations, such as the RSNA 2024 AI Challenge, these sample weights are explicitly codified into the competition's evaluation metric itself: a penalty weight of 1 is assigned to Normal/Mild errors, a weight of 2 is assigned to Moderate errors, and a severe penalty weight of 4 is assigned to Severe errors6. By directly integrating these exact mathematical coefficients into the model's training loss function, researchers ensure that the network prioritizes the accurate detection of severe stenosis, aligning the model's objective function directly with clinical priorities6.  
Beyond WCE, advanced studies frequently utilize Weighted Focal Loss. Focal loss introduces a dynamic modulating factor that scales the cross-entropy loss based on the model's current confidence in its prediction. It aggressively down-weights the loss assigned to well-classified, "easy" examples (typically the abundant Normal cases) and focuses the gradient updates almost entirely on hard-to-classify, borderline examples10. This is particularly crucial for distinguishing between Mild and Moderate stenosis, a boundary that suffers from extremely high inter-reader variability even among expert human radiologists.

### **Advanced Paradigm: Disc-Centric Contrastive Pretraining**

Pushing the boundary of imbalance mitigation, recent literature highlights the immense utility of disc-centric contrastive learning. As elegantly demonstrated by Acharya and Kansakar (2026), supervised training from scratch on imbalanced data often forces models to latch onto irrelevant differences in image appearance. By employing a contrastive pretraining phase on localized, cropped regions of interest (ROIs) centered specifically on the intervertebral discs, the network is trained to pull visually similar severity grades together in the latent feature space, while pushing differing grades apart, prior to any explicit classification20. This self-supervised clustering approach helps the network focus strictly on intrinsic, meaningful disc features. Subsequent supervised fine-tuning utilizing weighted focal loss on this pre-trained latent space demonstrated a highly robust 78.1% balanced accuracy, remarkably reducing the critical severe-to-normal misclassification rate to a mere 2.13% compared with models trained purely via supervised learning20.

## **The Impact of the RSNA 2024 Lumbar Spine Challenge (LumbarDISC)**

Historically, the rapid advancement of AI in spinal imaging was severely hindered by the fragmentation and siloing of proprietary medical datasets. Models trained on small, highly localized patient cohorts from single institutions frequently suffered from severe performance degradation—known as domain shift—when exposed to external data featuring different MRI scanners, varying imaging protocols, or diverse patient demographics4. This bottleneck was systematically dismantled by the release of the Radiological Society of North America (RSNA) Lumbar Degenerative Imaging Spine Classification (LumbarDISC) dataset.  
Published prominently and peer-reviewed in *Radiology: Artificial Intelligence* (Richards et al., 2026), LumbarDISC currently stands as the largest, most structurally diverse, and comprehensively annotated publicly available dataset for adult lumbar spine MRI ever assembled for degenerative disease9. The dataset represents a monumental collaboration, aggregating multisequence, multiplanar MRIs from 2,697 unique patients, comprising a total of 8,593 image series9. Crucially, to ensure robustness against domain shift, these images were sourced from eight distinct medical institutions spanning six countries and five continents9.  
The ground truth annotations establishing severity were generated through thousands of hours of manual labor by expert volunteer neuroradiologists and musculoskeletal radiologists representing the RSNA, the American Society of Neuroradiology (ASNR), and the American Society of Spine Radiology (ASSR)9. These experts meticulously graded the degree of spinal canal stenosis, subarticular recess stenosis, and neural foraminal stenosis at each of the five intervertebral disc levels (L1/L2 through L5/S1) on a standardized ordinal severity scale (Normal/Mild, Moderate, Severe)9.  
The introduction of LumbarDISC alongside the global RSNA 2024 AI Challenge profoundly standardized the evaluation of spine-centric artificial intelligence. By providing an open-source, globally diverse baseline, the dataset forcefully shifted the global research paradigm away from generic, whole-image classification toward anatomically localized, multi-task, disc-level severity grading. This catalyzed a massive surge of state-of-the-art methodological innovations between 2024 and 2026, redefining best practices in medical computer vision.

### **Standardization of Multi-Stage and Multi-View Architectures**

The top-performing frameworks evaluated on the LumbarDISC dataset almost universally adopted multi-stage pipelines, definitively and empirically proving that precise anatomical localization must precede severity classification for optimal diagnostic accuracy6. The inherent difficulty of the RSNA task lies in the spatial complexity: the network must identify five discrete discs across dozens of sequential slices in multiple imaging planes.  
A prime example of state-of-the-art innovation on this dataset is the M-SCAN framework (Batra et al., 2025), which introduced a multistage approach utilizing multi-view cross-attention to achieve an unprecedented Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC) of 0.97113. M-SCAN effectively operationalizes the cognitive workflow of a human radiologist by explicitly integrating multiplanar sequences (Sagittal T1, Sagittal T2, and Axial T2). In its primary stage, it utilizes a U-Net architecture with a ResNet50 backbone to extract precise spatial coordinates, localizing the spinal narrowing points in 3D space. It calculates the Z-coordinate distances to identify the exact axial slices that correspond geometrically to specific sagittal disc levels5.  
In the subsequent stages, CNN encoders extract high-dimensional features from these isolated, localized slices. Because an intervertebral disc spans multiple MRI slices, M-SCAN passes these features into complex temporal modeling layers—specifically Bidirectional Gated Recurrent Units (Bi-GRU) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks—to capture the 3D spatial dependencies across adjacent slices in the volume13. Ultimately, a sophisticated cross-attention mechanism dynamically aligns the sagittal and axial feature maps, allowing the network to weigh features based on their relevance across different MRI modalities before the final fully connected classification heads output the severity prediction13.  
Similarly, the highest-ranking winning solutions from the Kaggle RSNA 2024 challenge universally utilized complex ensembling and rigorous two-stage pipelines17. These pipelines typically deploy heavy object detection models (such as YOLOX or Faster R-CNN with Feature Pyramid Networks) in Stage 1 to generate bounding boxes or keypoint heatmaps for each of the five intervertebral discs on both Sagittal T1 and T2 sequences6. Stage 2 subsequently isolates these crops and utilizes 2.5D models (integrating 2D CNNs like EfficientNet with sequence models or attention-based Multiple Instance Learning) to analyze the volumetric depth of the disc and output the final classification17.

### **The Multi-Sequence vs. Single-Sequence Trade-off**

The introduction of the RSNA benchmark also vividly illuminated a critical divergence in model design regarding input data dependencies. While clinical gold standards clearly dictate the use of all three primary sequences (Sagittal T1 for NFN, Sagittal T2 for SCS, and Axial T2 for SAS) to achieve a definitive diagnosis, processing all three sequences simultaneously presents a massive engineering challenge5. It demands immense computational overhead, high GPU memory bandwidth, complex 3D coordinate registration between orthogonal planes, and prolonged inference times5.  
Consequently, researchers began actively exploring whether advanced deep learning architectures could infer sufficient diagnostic information from a reduced, highly optimized subset of sequences. Some highly ranked models demonstrated that utilizing both sagittal and axial sequences concurrently for spinal canal stenosis prediction significantly improved robustness and diagnostic confidence21. Conversely, other highly streamlined pipelines attempted to derive maximum clinical utility from just the sagittal sequences. For instance, specific Swin Transformer-based architectures were aggressively trained to classify all severities using solely Sagittal T1 and Sagittal T2 inputs. These models utilized the Vision Transformer's global attention mechanisms to infer 3D structural narrowing and central canal obliteration without explicitly loading the computationally heavy Axial T2 cross-sections6.  
This specific divergence underscores a vital, ongoing inquiry in medical AI: the optimization of diagnostic yield versus computational efficiency. If an advanced architecture can maintain high sensitivity and specificity for severe disease using a single, ubiquitous sequence (such as Sagittal T2, which is universally acquired in almost all back-pain protocols), it drastically reduces the computational infrastructure required for hospital deployment, paving the way for rapid, real-time clinical triage and resource optimization in low-resource medical environments.

## **Synthesis and Research Gap**

The systematic synthesis of recent literature reveals a highly mature but actively evolving field at the intersection of computer vision and musculoskeletal radiology. The historical transition from classical machine learning to deep Convolutional Neural Networks (e.g., ResNet50, EfficientNet-B4) enabled the automated, highly accurate extraction of localized anatomical features within the lumbar spine, effectively bridging the gap between theoretical AI and clinical viability5. Concurrently, the recent introduction of Vision Transformers, specifically the Swin Transformer, has offered a compelling and powerful architectural alternative. By mitigating the quadratic computational bottlenecks of standard Transformers via shifted window mechanisms, Swin Transformers excel in capturing both highly localized fine-grained details and global contextual dependencies across the entire spinal column18.  
Furthermore, the release of the massive, multi-institutional RSNA LumbarDISC dataset has unequivocally standardized the evaluation criteria for these diverse architectures. The benchmark demands that models move beyond simple binary classification of whole images, requiring them to accurately localize anatomy and grade severity across highly imbalanced classes at specific intervertebral levels, mirroring the precise workflow of human subspecialists9.  
Despite the proliferation of highly sophisticated multi-stage and multi-view architectures—such as M-SCAN—that synthesize data from all available MRI sequences (Sagittal T1, Sagittal T2, and Axial T2) to achieve maximal accuracy13, a critical and highly actionable gap remains in the literature regarding the optimization of computational efficiency for real-world clinical deployment. Multi-sequence models are computationally prohibitive; they require extensive spatial preprocessing, complex 3D coordinate mapping to align orthogonal planes, and immense GPU memory allocations that exceed the capabilities of standard hospital IT infrastructure.  
Crucially, the literature lacks a rigorous, controlled comparative experiment assessing whether the advanced global attention mechanisms inherent to hierarchical Vision Transformers (like the Swin Transformer) can adequately compensate for the absence of multiplanar spatial data when compared directly against optimized CNNs (like ResNet50 and EfficientNet-B4). Therefore, the explicit gap addressed by this thesis is the systematic evaluation and comparison of CNN architectures versus the Swin Transformer in an end-to-end severity classification pipeline, specifically contrasting model performance when utilizing a comprehensive, computationally heavy multi-sequence input (Sagittal T1, Sagittal T2, Axial T2) versus a highly efficient, single-sequence (Sagittal T2) input. By leveraging the standardized LumbarDISC dataset and sophisticated class-imbalance mitigation strategies (such as class-weighted focal loss and weighted random sampling), this study seeks to definitively determine if computational efficiency can be maximized via a single-sequence approach without sacrificing the critical diagnostic accuracy required for automated degenerative disease triage.
